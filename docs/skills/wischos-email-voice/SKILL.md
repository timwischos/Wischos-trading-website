# Wischos Email Voice — John's Style Guide

A reference for writing, reviewing, and polishing John's outbound B2B emails. This voice has been built over real customer interactions (Argos, BlueShark, others) and tuned for the NZ/AU/UK/SG business cultures where Wischos Gift operates.

## When to use this skill

Trigger when:
- John asks to draft, write, optimize, or review a sales/follow-up/outreach email
- John shares a draft and asks for feedback
- John asks "how should I reply to this customer?"
- A specific paragraph or phrase needs reworking

Do NOT use for:
- Internal Chinese-language messages (factories, suppliers, partners in China)
- Personal correspondence
- Marketing copy (website, brochures, social media — different voice register)

---

## Voice Profile

**Positioning in one sentence:**
> Understated, relationship-led, information-seeking — never pushy, never bragging, never desperate.

**5 core characteristics:**

1. **Acknowledge first, advocate second.** Every reply starts by validating the customer's position before stating his own.
2. **Light-touch sales — almost no push.** No "please consider us" / "hope to hear from you" / "let me know if interested." Lets the ball sit with the customer naturally.
3. **Questions over pitches.** Information asks drive the conversation more than product features.
4. **Short paragraphs, low information density per line.** Maximum 2-3 lines per paragraph. Total email typically under 120 words.
5. **Future hooks, not present pressure.** Uses "another day" / "for our own calibration" / "if a brief comes up later" — opens future doors without pushing today.

## Core Principles (with examples)

### Principle 1 — Empathy first

Before stating any position, validate the customer's worldview.

✅ Good:
> "It makes sense that an experienced team like yours has built a solid local supplier network..."

❌ Avoid:
> "I understand you have other options, but here's why we're different..."

### Principle 2 — Never push, never beg

No urgency language. No "please" stacking. No "we really hope."

✅ Good:
> "If any of those come up later, happy to revisit."

❌ Avoid:
> "Please let me know if you have any questions. We hope to hear from you soon."

### Principle 3 — Ask, don't tell

Use questions as the primary engagement driver. Most John emails contain 1-2 specific information asks.

✅ Good:
> "When you import, which Incoterm do you typically prefer to work with?"

❌ Avoid:
> "We offer FOB, CIF, and DDP — all of which give you flexibility."

### Principle 4 — Show, don't sell quality

Never make qualitative claims about Wischos's superiority. Use specific spec language or position by use case instead.

✅ Good:
> "Where the gap typically narrows is on long-run orders or recurring programs."

❌ Avoid:
> "Our quality is higher than local catalog options."

### Principle 5 — Specific over abstract

Names of techniques, materials, or formats over generic adjectives.

✅ Good:
> "Brass crown, anodised aluminium body, stainless steel clip."

❌ Avoid:
> "Premium-feel pen with quality construction."

### Principle 6 — Leave the exit door open

Always give the recipient a graceful out. Make declining easy and face-saving.

✅ Good:
> "Completely fine if you'd rather not."

❌ Avoid:
> "Looking forward to your reply."

## Email Formula (the standard structure)

Most John follow-up / sales emails follow this 4-part structure:

```
1. Acknowledge (1 line)
   → Validate the customer's recent message or position

2. Common ground (1-2 lines)
   → Find a point of agreement / shared reality

3. Where Wischos fits (1-2 lines, optional)
   → Position scenarios where the relationship adds value
   → Use "longer runs / recurring programs / specific spec briefs"
   → End with "but that's for another day" to release pressure

4. Ask (1-2 specific questions OR sign-off)
   → Information ask framed for "future calibration"
   → Give the recipient an easy out

Sign off: "Best regards, John"
No PS. No CTA buttons. No links.
```

**Total target: 80-120 words.**

## Phrase Library

### Preferred phrases (use these)

| Function | Preferred |
|---|---|
| Validate customer | "It makes sense that..." / "Understood that..." / "Appreciate the directness." |
| Acknowledge price feedback | "Freight does load more cost onto each set." / "Smaller volumes carry a structural disadvantage on freight." |
| Position Wischos fit | "Where we tend to be useful..." / "Where the gap typically narrows..." |
| Future hook | "...but that's for another day." / "If a brief comes up later, happy to revisit." |
| Ask for info | "If you'd be open to sharing..." / "For our own calibration..." / "Even directional input helps." |
| Soft close | "Completely fine if you'd rather not." / "No urgency from our side." |
| Soft refusal | "We tend not to..." / "Our standard approach is..." |

### Avoid these phrases (don't use)

| Don't use | Why |
|---|---|
| "Please consider us" | Begging, signals weakness |
| "We hope to hear from you soon" | Pressure, signals neediness |
| "Looking forward to your reply" | Cliche, transactional |
| "Our quality is superior" | Bragging without proof |
| "Compared to other suppliers" | Punching at competitors |
| "Don't hesitate to reach out" | Cliche, hollow |
| "Win-win" / "synergy" | Corporate buzzword fatigue |
| "Kindly do the needful" | Reads as Indian English boilerplate |
| Multiple exclamation marks | Reads as inexperienced |
| Heavy "please" stacking | Submissive register |

### Industry vocabulary (use when relevant)

These signal "John understands the trade":

- **Long-run orders** / **longer runs** — larger production quantities per PO
- **Recurring programs** — repeat-pattern client engagements
- **Locked pricing** — multi-period price agreements
- **Setup amortisation** — one-time costs spread over volume
- **Reference samples** — non-branded inspection samples
- **Custom samples** — with logo applied
- **Mill spec / material certificate** — for material verification briefs
- **Spec stability** — same spec across reorders
- **Forecast / production slots** — capacity planning
- **Incoterm (FOB / CIF / DAP / DDP)** — always use full name on first mention

## Product Claim Validation — The MOQ 100 Economic Check

Wischos's typical inbound is small-batch B2B distributors working at MOQ 50-500. Any email that names a specific product or capability ("we can do X") must pre-validate at the **MOQ 100 economic floor** before sending.

### Why this rule exists

Aspirational claims that can't be backed at MOQ 100 cause compounding damage:
- First-quote credibility loss ("you said 100 was fine, now you say 300?")
- Permanently weaker negotiating position on every future deal with that prospect
- Lost trust with Argos-type customers — they remember inconsistencies
- Damaged reputation when distributors compare notes within their industry network

One walked-back MOQ destroys 6 months of careful relationship-building.

### The 4-question check

For every concrete product/capability claim in an email, run through:

1. **Does Wischos have a confirmed factory partner that quotes this at MOQ 100?**
2. **What's the realistic FOB per unit at MOQ 100** — including logo, packaging, and tooling amortization?
3. **Does this require tooling / mold / setup that breaks economics at low volumes?**
4. **If MOQ 100 doesn't work — what's the honest minimum?** (300, 500, 1000)

### Three outcomes after the check

| Outcome | What to do |
|---|---|
| ✅ **Works cleanly at MOQ 100** | State plainly: "MOQ 100, FOB USD X-Y" |
| ⚠️ **Works at 100 but expensive / requires note** | State with MOQ note inline: "MOQ 100 for engraved pieces; die-cast custom shapes from 300+" |
| ❌ **Doesn't work at 100** | Either state the honest MOQ explicitly OR drop the claim entirely |

### Quick reference: typical MOQ realities for common Wischos products

| Product type | Lowest workable MOQ | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Existing-catalog brass pen | 100 | In-catalog, no setup |
| Brass pen with custom shape / new body | 500-1000 | New tooling required |
| Engraved brass key tag / plaque | 100 | Laser engraving, no mold |
| CNC desk piece (existing SKU) | 100 | In-catalog |
| CNC custom small piece (no mold) | 100 | Direct CNC machining |
| Soft-enamel lapel pin (custom shape) | 100 | Small mold, USD 30-80 mold cost |
| Photo-etched brass pin (flat) | 100 | No mold |
| Die-cast zinc alloy 3D keepsake | 300 | USD 300-800 mold cost |
| Die-cast medal (standard ribbon style) | 300 | Same |
| Stainless steel / titanium drinkware (existing) | 100 | In-catalog |
| New custom drinkware mold | 1000-3000 | Major tooling investment |
| Standard packaging (in-stock box sizes) | 100 | Stock dielines |
| Custom rigid gift box (new dieline) | 300-500 | New dieline + plate setup |

This table is the operational baseline. When in doubt, default to the safer (higher) MOQ in your claim; raise expectations later if economics improve.

### Anti-patterns

- ❌ "We can do any custom shape at 100" — false for die-cast
- ❌ Using "custom" without specifying tooling implications
- ❌ Quoting MOQ 100 then walking it back at quote stage
- ❌ Burying the MOQ caveat in a later paragraph — put it inline with the claim

### Correct patterns

- ✅ "Engraved pieces from MOQ 100; die-cast custom shapes from 300+"
- ✅ "Catalogue items at MOQ 100; new tooling pieces from 300/500/1000 depending on type"
- ✅ "If a brief requires a custom mold, we'll set realistic MOQ expectations upfront"
- ✅ "For larger development handovers, a custom die-cast keepsake works cleanly from 300+"

### Worked example (from real outreach to Echo Corporate Supplies, 2026-06)

**Original draft:**
> "...custom-shaped metal piece (brass key tag, house-outline keepsake)..."

**Problem identified:** "house-outline keepsake" implies die-cast (MOQ 300+), but it was bundled with brass key tag (MOQ 100 OK) in the same sentence — inconsistent MOQ implication. The prospect would assume 100 works for everything listed, leading to walked-back-MOQ damage at quote stage.

**Fixed version:**
> "...a brass key tag (laser-cut + engraved), a property-monogrammed desk plaque, or for larger development handovers, a custom die-cast house-outline keepsake. **MOQ 100 for engraved pieces; die-cast custom shapes work cleanly from 300+.**"

**Result:** Three product options across two clear MOQ tiers. Prospect can self-select based on project size. No walked-back-MOQ risk later. Demonstrates Wischos understands its own production economics — which itself is a credibility signal.

## Pre-Send Checklist

Before sending any email, run through this 8-point check:

1. **Word count under 120?** If over, cut the weakest paragraph. (Up to 250 OK if recipient specifically invited materials.)
2. **Any "please" stacking?** Replace with neutral phrasing.
3. **Any pushy CTAs?** ("Looking forward" / "Hope to hear" → delete)
4. **Did I acknowledge before advocating?** First paragraph should validate, not pitch.
5. **Is there at least one information ask?** Without it, the email is one-way.
6. **Did I leave a graceful exit?** Recipient must be able to decline without feeling rude.
7. **Grammarly / spell-check passed?** Typos are John's most consistent leakage point.
8. **MOQ 100 economic check passed?** Every product/capability claim verified against the real factory baseline at the stated MOQ — see "Product Claim Validation" section above. Any walked-back MOQ at quote stage destroys 6 months of relationship-building.

## Customer-Type Calibration

John's voice is well-tuned for these segments. Mild adjustments when needed:

| Customer | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| **NZ / AU mid-tier distributor** | No adjustment — native fit |
| **UK procurement-led buyer** | Slightly more formal sign-off ("Kind regards" instead of "Best regards") |
| **SG MNC procurement specialist** | Slightly more direct on technical specs; less narrative |
| **Dubai / UAE gifting agency** | Slightly warmer opening ("Trust this finds you well" optional) |
| **US distributor** (rare for Wischos) | Lighter on understatement, slightly more enthusiastic tone |
| **HK Chinese-leaning buyer** | Can mix bilingual phrasing if appropriate |

Do NOT switch voice to match aggressive / hard-sell cultures (e.g., India trading hubs, US affiliate marketing). John's understated voice is a differentiator — preserve it.

## Common Scenarios + Mini Templates

### Scenario A — Replying to a polite brush-off (price too high)

```
Hi [name],

Thank you for the honest feedback.

Understood that this quote doesn't fit on this occasion. 
It also makes sense that an experienced team like yours 
has built a solid local supplier network delivering 
similar products at competitive prices.

Where the gap typically narrows is on longer runs or 
recurring programs — but that's for another day.

If you'd be open to sharing the rough gap between our 
quote and your expectation, it would help us calibrate 
better on future enquiries.

One more thing, if you don't mind — when you import, 
which Incoterm do you typically prefer to work with?

Best regards,
John
```

### Scenario B — Sending a first quotation (after delay)

```
Hi [name],

Thank you for your interest in [products], [qty] to [destination].

I took a couple of days to verify the component specs 
and the LCL freight assumptions to [destination] before 
coming back to you — I didn't want to send a rough number 
that would shift once freight was included.

Quotation attached in three Incoterms for your reference:
  – FOB [origin]
  – CIF [destination port]
  – DAP [destination address]

Feel free to share your thoughts once you've had a look.

Best regards,
John
```

### Scenario C — Cold first outreach to a new distributor

```
Hi [name],

Saw [their company] serves [their market segment] — 
[specific detail from their website].

Where we'd be useful: when a client brief calls for 
considered metal pieces (executive, VIP, milestone 
gifting) rather than catalog drinkware, our curated

Capability sheet attached for your reference. No urgency, 
just want to be on file when a metal-leaning brief

Best regards,
John
Wischos Gift
wischosgift.com
```

### Scenario D — Long-line follow-up (6+ weeks after lost deal)

```
Hi [name],

Quick note — [observation about something they've posted

We've recently put together [new SKU / new program /
```

### Scenario E — First email after warm LinkedIn intro (prospect invited materials)

**When to use:**
- Prospect accepted LinkedIn connection
- Prospect explicitly invited you to email / send the catalogue
- This is the first email after the LinkedIn warm-up
- Most common scenario in the prospect-pain-discovery + outreach pipeline

**Default version — use for 80% of cases (~175 words):**

```
Hi [name],

Thanks for connecting and sharing your email.

The website covers our core range; the catalogue attached 
gives you a fuller picture.

We specialise in custom metal gift sets and metal promotional 
items — pens, desk pieces, drinkware, EDC, lapel pins, medals 
and custom-shaped pieces — sourced from China for planned B2B 
projects, both catalogue and off-catalogue.

The catalogue is intentionally focused rather than broad. 
We're not trying to compete as a general commodity supplier — 
the focus is selected metal corporate gifts, custom gift set 
concepts, and items that adapt cleanly for branding, packaging 
and distributor-led client work. Even when a set includes 
non-metal items, the metal pieces hold the primary role — 
that's the consistent thread.

This shows up in two ways: as complete metal-led gift sets 
we build to brief, or as metal components inside your existing 
programs.

If any of this fits a current project, happy to put together 
a short concept sheet with specific options — no commitment 
needed.

Best regards,
John
Wischos Gift
wischosgift.com
```

**Why this is the default:**

The default version contains:
- Brand identity (metal-first — non-speculative)
- Two-mode positioning (sets-first, components-second — preserves Wischos as set-builder)
- Catalogue handoff (lets the attached PDF do the heavy lifting)
- Open invitation (no pressure, no projection of recipient's needs)

It deliberately does NOT include speculative angles about the prospect's current business — because every guess is a chance to misalign and every page of speculation is a page Tom/Andrew/etc. doesn't have to read.

### Scenario E — UPGRADE version (with 3 specific angles)

**Use the UPGRADE only when ALL of these are true:**

1. You did **fresh web research** on the prospect's site (within last 24 hours)
2. You can name **2-3 specific collections / sub-brands / SKU names** from their site (e.g., "GIFT'D Settlement Gifts", "Workplace On-Boarding Sets", "Harmony Cardboard Gift Set")
3. Prospect is **Director-led at mid/large company** AND said "I will look at your website" (signalling they want guidance on what to look at)
4. **MOQ 100 economic check passed** for every product mentioned in the angles (see Product Claim Validation section)
5. Each angle reference is **verifiable** by clicking a page on their site

If ANY condition fails → use the default version. Do not fake specificity.

**Template structure for UPGRADE version:**

Insert 3 angles between the "two ways" sentence and the close:

```
A few directions that may fit what [their company] handles:

1. For [their specific collection name] — [their target 
   customers from their website] — [Wischos product fit + 
   MOQ tier]. [Tooling/MOQ note if relevant].

2. For [their specific collection/SKU name] — [reference 
   their current format like "Notebook + Pen + foam box"] — 
   [Wischos upgrade/component angle].

3. For [program type they actually run] that sit alongside 
   the standard collections — [Wischos product line + MOQ 
   tier]. [FTA / cert / other technical anchor if relevant].
```

**Real-world calibration examples:**

| Prospect | Used | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Tom Rogic (Echo, AU) | UPGRADE version | Fresh research done; cited GIFT'D Settlement Gifts + Workplace On-Boarding + Harmony + Anthem by name; Tom said "I will jump onto your website" — wanted guidance |
| Andrew Waldman (All About Promo, NZ) | DEFAULT version (in hindsight) | Smaller owner-led casual company; Andrew asked "send a catalogue or website link" — wanted materials not propositions; angles would have been speculation |

**Decision rule in one line:**

> **Specific angles only when you can quote the prospect's own site back to them.** Otherwise, default version. Speculation is not specificity.

## Style "tells" to watch for in John's drafts

When reviewing John's own writing, watch for these typical leakage points:

| Leakage | Fix |
|---|---|
| "It's pity to know..." | "Understood that..." |
| "Would you please share with me..." | "If you'd be open to sharing..." |
| "Kindly + verb" stacking | Drop the kindly, keep the verb |
| "pay attention on X" | "pay attention to X" (Chinese-English carry-over) |
| "potiential / exceptation / feeback" | Run Grammarly always |
| "We would like to..." | "Happy to..." / "We can..." |
| Long sentences with multiple "and" | Break into 2-3 shorter sentences |
| Missing comma after introductory phrase | Add comma |

## When to break the rules

These guidelines are defaults, not laws. Break them when:

- **The customer has shown strong engagement** → can be slightly more direct
- **The customer asked a yes/no question** → answer plainly first, voice second
- **You're confirming an order** → drop the soft hooks, be precise and operational
- **The customer used aggressive language** → match firmness without matching aggression
- **It's a quotation cover** → can be slightly more formal/structured

> **Validate first. Ask more than you tell. Leave the door open. Never push.**

If a draft fails any of those four, rework it before sending.
